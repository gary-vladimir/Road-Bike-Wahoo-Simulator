For road cycling, most workouts are built around the same physiological systems, whether you ride indoors on your KICKR or outdoors. The biggest difference is **how you combine them for triathlon versus cycling-only racing**.

A useful way to think about cycling training is this:

| Workout type           | Main purpose                   | Typical intensity | Triathlon | Bike racing |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------: | :-------: | :---------: |
| Recovery ride          | Circulation, recovery          |                Z1 |    ✅     |     ✅      |
| Endurance / Zone 2     | Aerobic engine                 |                Z2 |  ⭐⭐⭐   |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Tempo                  | Sustained moderate power       |                Z3 |  ⭐⭐⭐   |    ⭐⭐     |
| Sweet Spot             | Strong aerobic power           |       ~88–94% FTP |  ⭐⭐⭐   |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Threshold              | Raise FTP                      |      ~95–105% FTP |  ⭐⭐⭐   |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| VO₂max                 | Increase aerobic ceiling       |     ~106–120% FTP |   ⭐⭐    |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Anaerobic intervals    | Short explosive efforts        |         120%+ FTP |    ⭐     |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Sprint / neuromuscular | Max acceleration/power         |        Max effort |    ⭐     |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Low-cadence / torque   | Cycling-specific strength      |    Moderate power |  ⭐⭐⭐   |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| High-cadence drills    | Pedal efficiency               |     Easy–moderate |   ⭐⭐    |    ⭐⭐     |
| Hill repeats           | Strength + aerobic power       |          Variable |   ⭐⭐    |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Brick workout          | Bike → run adaptation          |     Race specific |  ⭐⭐⭐   |      —      |
| Race-pace ride         | Specific pacing                |    Race intensity |  ⭐⭐⭐   |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Long ride              | Durability + fueling           |         Mostly Z2 |  ⭐⭐⭐   |   ⭐⭐⭐    |
| Skills / handling      | Cornering, braking, descending |          Variable |   ⭐⭐    |   ⭐⭐⭐    |

### 1. Recovery rides

Very easy cycling, usually **30–60 minutes**.

Think:

> Easy spinning, almost embarrassingly easy.

You're not trying to improve fitness directly. You're facilitating recovery between harder sessions.

Typical cadence: **85–95 rpm**.

For an indoor trainer, this is perfect because you can precisely keep the effort low.

---

### 2. Endurance / Zone 2

This is probably the **single most important cycling workout for a triathlete**.

Typical session:

**60 min–5+ hours at Zone 2**

Roughly:

**55–75% FTP**

or an intensity where you could comfortably hold a conversation.

It develops:

- mitochondrial density
- fat oxidation
- aerobic efficiency
- muscular endurance
- ability to ride for several hours
- ability to recover from harder training

A triathlete might do enormous amounts of Z2.

Cyclists also do a lot of it, but their races may require much more explosive work layered on top.

---

### 3. Tempo

One step harder than endurance.

Approximately:

**76–87% FTP**

Example:

**3 × 20 min tempo**
with 5 min easy between.

Tempo teaches you to maintain relatively high power without accumulating fatigue as quickly as threshold work.

It's very useful for:

- Olympic triathlon
- 70.3
- long climbs
- long steady road riding.

---

### 4. Sweet Spot

One of the most popular structured cycling workouts.

Approximately:

**88–94% FTP**

Example:

**3 × 15 min @ 90% FTP**

or eventually:

**2 × 30 min**

Sweet Spot sits just below threshold and gives a large aerobic training stimulus without being as exhausting.

For someone training seriously but also swimming and running, it's particularly useful because you're getting a lot of cycling stimulus **without destroying your legs**.

---

### 5. Threshold / FTP workouts

Threshold is approximately the hardest power you can sustain for a prolonged period.

Approximately:

**95–105% FTP**

Examples:

**4 × 8 min @ 100% FTP**

or

**3 × 12 min @ 100% FTP**

or

**2 × 20 min @ 95–100% FTP**

Threshold training improves your FTP.

Higher FTP means almost everything below FTP becomes relatively easier.

For example, imagine your FTP is:

**200 W**

and your triathlon race power is:

**160 W**

That's 80% FTP.

If your FTP later becomes:

**250 W**

then the same 160 W is only:

**64% FTP**.

That is a huge difference.

---

### 6. VO₂max intervals

Hard.

Usually:

**3–6 minute intervals**

at approximately:

**106–120% FTP**

Example:

**5 × 4 min @ 115% FTP**
with 4 min recovery.

These increase your aerobic ceiling—your maximum oxygen-processing ability.

They're important for both cyclists and triathletes, although pure cyclists usually emphasize them more.

---

### 7. Anaerobic intervals

Now you're going above the aerobic system.

Examples:

**30 sec hard / 30 sec easy × 10**

or

**6 × 1 min @ 140% FTP**

These train your ability to attack, accelerate, bridge gaps and survive extremely hard efforts.

Very important for:

- criteriums
- road racing
- breakaways
- short hills
- attacks.

Much less important for long-distance triathlon.

---

### 8. Sprint / neuromuscular training

These are extremely short, maximal efforts.

For example:

**6 × 10-second maximal sprint**

with **3–5 minutes recovery**.

You're training:

- peak power
- recruitment of fast-twitch fibers
- acceleration
- coordination.

A cyclist racing criteriums or road races may care a lot about this.

A long-course triathlete usually doesn't.

---

## 9. Torque / low-cadence training

This one is particularly useful indoors.

Example:

**5 × 5 min**

at moderate/high power at:

**55–65 rpm**

instead of your normal 85–95 rpm.

It forces you to produce more torque each pedal stroke.

It can help develop cycling-specific muscular strength.

Just don't turn it into extremely heavy grinding.

---

## 10. Cadence drills

The opposite.

For example:

**5 × 1 min at 110–120 rpm**

while keeping power relatively low.

These train:

- smooth pedaling
- neuromuscular coordination
- cadence control.

You can also practice changing gears and cadence, which is useful for the realistic indoor training you were interested in.

---

# Then there are race-specific workouts

This is where **triathlon training and bicycle racing begin to diverge substantially.**

## Triathlon cycling

Triathlon rewards something unusual:

### being boringly consistent.

You don't normally want this:

**150 W → 400 W → 120 W → 500 W → 170 W**

You want something closer to:

**210 → 215 → 205 → 212 → 208 W**

Because every unnecessary acceleration burns energy that you'll need for the run.

So triathlon-specific cycling includes several special workouts.

### Race-pace intervals

Example for Olympic triathlon:

**3 × 20 min at predicted race power**

You practice exactly the intensity you'll hold during the race.

---

### Brick workouts

Probably the most uniquely triathlon-specific workout.

Example:

**90 min bike**

followed immediately by:

**20 min run**

This trains your body to transition from cycling to running.

Your legs initially feel strange because the neuromuscular pattern changes dramatically.

---

### Long bike + short run

A classic triathlon session might be:

**3 h bike Z2**

- **20–30 min run**

The run doesn't necessarily need to be hard.

The purpose is learning to run efficiently after accumulating cycling fatigue.

---

### Aero-position training

For triathlon bikes especially, you need to learn to produce power while staying aerodynamic.

Even on your current road bike, eventually you can work on:

- stable torso
- relaxed shoulders
- efficient position
- holding power without constantly moving.

---

### Fueling workouts

Extremely important.

Long rides become nutrition experiments.

You practice:

- carbohydrates/hour
- fluids
- sodium
- gels
- bottles.

You don't want race day to be the first time you discover your stomach doesn't tolerate a particular fueling strategy.

---

# Cycling-only road training

Road racing has some additional demands that triathlon doesn't.

Imagine a race:

easy → easy → **ATTACK** → easy → climb → **ATTACK** → descent → **SPRINT**.

Therefore cyclists train much more variability.

Common workouts include:

### Over-unders

Example:

2 min **105% FTP**

2 min **90% FTP**

repeated several times.

This teaches your body to recover while still riding hard.

Very useful in road racing.

---

### Attacks

Example:

Ride at 80% FTP.

Then suddenly:

**30 sec at 150% FTP.**

Return to tempo.

Repeat.

This simulates attacks in a peloton.

---

### Sprint finishes

Example:

90 min endurance ride

then:

**5 × 10 sec maximum sprint**

This simulates finishing a race while already tired.

---

### Hill repeats

For example:

**6 × 5 min uphill**

Hard climbing develops both aerobic power and strength.

---

### Group riding

Something no indoor trainer completely reproduces.

You learn:

- drafting
- positioning
- riding a wheel
- rotating pace lines
- cornering
- braking
- responding to attacks
- riding safely in close proximity.

For cycling competition, these skills can be almost as important as fitness.

---

# Indoor vs outdoor training

For your KICKR setup, indoor training is actually **better for several workout types** because there's no traffic, coasting or interruptions.

Indoor is excellent for:

**Sweet Spot
Threshold
VO₂max
Cadence drills
Low-cadence torque
Recovery
Structured intervals**

Outdoor is better for:

**Long endurance rides
Bike handling
Cornering
Descending
Climbing technique
Wind management
Gear selection
Real-world pacing**

For you specifically, since you're deliberately doing most of your cycling training indoors, you can develop **a very large percentage of the physiological fitness on the KICKR**. Outdoor sessions mainly become important for acquiring the technical skills that an indoor trainer can't reproduce.

---

# The training pyramid I'd use for a triathlete

If we simplify everything, think of your cycling development as:

**Base**

Zone 2 endurance

⬇

**Muscular endurance**

Tempo / Sweet Spot

⬇

**FTP**

Threshold

⬇

**Aerobic ceiling**

VO₂max

⬇

**Race specificity**

Race pace + aero + fueling

⬇

**Triathlon specificity**

Bike → Run bricks

For someone pursuing triathlon seriously, you don't need to constantly smash yourself with insane cycling workouts. A huge part of becoming fast is developing the ability to produce **high, steady power for a long time while still getting off the bike capable of running fast**.

And that's one of the biggest conceptual differences between **being a great cyclist** and **being a great triathlon cyclist**.
